## One-sentence summary

FLORES uses a “front steering, rear support” wheel-leg configuration to turn and move laterally without repeatedly lifting the heavy front wheel modules.

## Problem addressed

![FLORES mechanical design and joint degrees of freedom.](/reviews/2026-07-22-FLORES/fig1-hardware-design.png)

*Figure 1. FLORES mechanical design and joint degrees of freedom.*

Conventional wheeled-legged robots either use active suspensions with restricted dynamic capability or add wheels to quadruped feet. The latter remain efficient in straight-line rolling but often require repeated leg lifting for turns and lateral motion, making wheel mass an energetic disadvantage. Controllers also commonly treat driving and stepping as separate modes.

The paper investigates the co-design of mechanical configuration and learned control to preserve wheel efficiency while retaining legged adaptability.

## Main contribution

- FLORES, a 16-DoF robot with front hip-yaw and rear hip-roll joints.
- A tailored HIM-PPO controller for multimodal locomotion and smooth transitions.
- Quantitative CoT comparisons with Unitree B2W in straight, lateral, turning, and path-following tasks.
- Open-source mechanical, electrical, and controller designs.

## Methodology

![Two-stage training, sim-to-real control, and hardware framework.](/reviews/2026-07-22-FLORES/fig2-system-framework.png)

*Figure 2. Two-stage training, sim-to-real control, and hardware framework.*

Each FLORES leg has four actuators. The front hip-yaw joints act as steering modules, while the rear hip-roll joints retain lateral support and stepping capability. Low-ratio planetary transmissions support high speed, impact resistance, and current-based torque estimation.

The controller uses a 689-dimensional observation containing 12 history frames. A HIM policy trained with PPO outputs 12 leg-position offsets and four wheel velocities. Training runs for 18 hours in 4,096 Isaac Gym environments, followed by MuJoCo validation and zero-shot ROS 2 deployment at 50 Hz.

## Experimental setup

Hardware tests cover paved ground, grass, gravel, discrete obstacles, slopes, and stairs. Unitree B2W is used as the comparison platform. Cost of transport is computed from positive mechanical power, with compensation for the B2W wheel-seal friction.

Tasks include 0.5–1.5 m/s straight motion, 0.5 m/s lateral motion, circular paths with 0.5–2.0 m radii, and an obstacle path with repeated 90-degree turns.

## Key results

![Real-world locomotion across terrains and cost-of-transport comparison.](/reviews/2026-07-22-FLORES/fig3-locomotion-results.png)

*Figure 3. Real-world locomotion across terrains and cost-of-transport comparison.*

In lateral motion, FLORES obtains a CoT of `0.3614`, compared with `0.6767` for B2W. For circular radii of `0.5/1.0/1.5/2.0 m`, FLORES achieves CoT values of `0.24/0.18/0.149/0.139`, all below B2W's `0.357/0.256/0.195/0.183`.

On the cluttered path, FLORES's instantaneous CoT at the turning points is approximately 70% of B2W's. It also retains hybrid locomotion capability on stairs, slopes, and loose terrain.

## Strengths

- The mechanical design directly targets steering inefficiency.
- Front and rear legs have clear complementary functions.
- CoT comparisons cover multiple motion modes and correct for baseline seal friction.
- The complete platform is open source.

## Weaknesses

- Hardware and controller change together, so their independent contributions are not fully isolated.
- The primary comparison uses one commercial platform.
- Front-rear asymmetry may increase modeling, maintenance, and recovery complexity.

## Discussion questions

1. Does the front hip-yaw design reduce capability for any purely legged maneuvers?
2. How can hardware and controller contributions be separated experimentally?
3. How does the asymmetric morphology affect fall recovery and whole-body manipulation?
4. Does the CoT advantage persist under payload and long-duration operation?

## Relevance to our lab

The paper is relevant to industrial wheeled humanoids, mobile-manipulator embodiment design, wheel-leg coordination, and energy-efficient motion. Its “optimize the structure first, amplify it with learning” approach is informative for high-speed, long-endurance mobile bases.

## Possible follow-up ideas

- Perform controller ablations on fixed hardware and morphology ablations under a common controller.
- Add payload, thermal, and endurance testing.
- Integrate manipulation, fall recovery, and autonomous navigation.
- Develop whole-body control explicitly accounting for front-rear asymmetry.
