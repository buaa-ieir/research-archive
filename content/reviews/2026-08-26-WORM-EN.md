## One-sentence summary

WORM lets artists program a collaborative robot through the manual craft actions they already know: the operator physically guides the robot and tool, records the motion, and then uses the computational system to repeat, transform, and combine it.

![WORM workflow](/reviews/2026-08-26-WORM-EN/fig1-workflow.png)

*Figure 1. Artists manipulate the cobot end effector through a handle controller, record actions, and generate repeated fabrication patterns.*

## Problem addressed

Collaborative robots are promising for low-volume and flexible digital fabrication, but typical programming workflows require symbolic programming, CAD/CAM, or point-based teaching. These workflows conflict with craft practice, which relies on material feedback, bodily motion, and continuous adjustment. The paper asks how a robotic fabrication interface can preserve manual craft knowledge while still enabling computational repetition and automation.

## Main contributions

- Introduces the **Workflow-Oriented Robotic Manufacturing (WORM)** system;
- treats craft actions as first-class programming datatypes;
- provides a custom modular handle controller for direct tool-like interaction with a UR10e cobot;
- defines four interaction modes: spatial information acquisition, action recording, action playback, and fixed-path operation;
- evaluates the system through need-finding interviews, a painter study, exemplar artifacts, and ceramicist expert evaluation.

## System design

![Interface and interaction](/reviews/2026-08-26-WORM-EN/fig2-interface.png)

*Figure 2. The desktop interface and handle controller support action recording, parameterized repetition, and execution control.*

The system does not try to replace artists with automatic design generation. Instead, it lets them capture what their bodies already know how to do: a brush stroke, cutting gesture, or surface-texturing action. Once recorded, the action can be offset, repeated, and recombined. The robot provides precision and repetition, while the artist preserves judgment over movement and material response.

## Key ideas

The paper proposes three design guidelines for future craft-aligned robotic fabrication systems: map programming datatypes to physical counterparts; maintain a short design-to-fabrication feedback loop with tool changes; and avoid reintroducing CAD/CAM conventions into physical workflows. In other words, the robot interface should amplify craft practice rather than translating it entirely into conventional engineering software.

## Evidence quality and academic assessment

WORM is an HCI / creativity-support systems paper, so it should not be judged as if it were a robot-control benchmark paper. The authors combine **need-finding interviews, a working prototype, exemplar artifact production, a preliminary expert study, and an in-depth expert evaluation**. Two professional ceramicists inform the design; a professional painter participates in the preliminary study; four exemplar artifacts probe the workflow across techniques; and a professional ceramicist completes extended fabrication activities and a semi-structured interview. In one activity, the expert records 14 carving actions and asks the robot to replay actions seven times while using around-axis, along-spine, and mirror operations.

This evidence is appropriate for questions about workflow fit, division of labor, and interaction abstractions, but it does not establish population-level efficiency or learning-curve superiority. The paper explicitly acknowledges that the evaluation involves only two professional artists and that the preliminary participant is related to one author, creating possible social-desirability bias. System limitations include accumulated error from manually defined offsets, disruption caused by switching between the handle and desktop interface, and the current emphasis on translational rather than full 6-DoF tool control. WORM’s strongest academic contribution is therefore the **interaction paradigm of treating craft actions as first-class programming datatypes**, not a quantitative claim that it outperforms CAD/CAM or conventional programming-by-demonstration.

## Strengths

- Starts from expert craft practice rather than generic robot automation;
- the “craft action” datatype is conceptually clear and transferable;
- links bodily movement, material feedback, and computational repetition;
- prototype artifacts make the contribution concrete;
- broadly relevant to end-user cobot programming.

## Limitations

- Evaluation is small-scale and exploratory;
- the system still relies partly on a desktop interface, which can interrupt physical engagement;
- manually defined offsets can accumulate error during repetition;
- applicability to industrial high-throughput production remains untested.

## Discussion questions

1. Should robot programming prioritize exact control or bodily expressiveness?
2. Can craft actions become a general representation for robot demonstration learning?
3. How should systems balance manual variability with robotic precision?
4. Should the desktop interface move to the handle, AR, or speech interaction?
5. Can WORM-like interfaces help collect better teleoperation and skill-learning data?

## Relation to our lab

WORM is relevant to teleoperation, learning from demonstration, and human-robot collaboration interfaces. It suggests that demonstrations should not only record trajectories, but also encode the operator’s material engagement and low-level action primitives. That idea could be useful for building robot skill libraries from human demonstrations.

## Possible follow-up directions

- combine craft-action recording with imitation-learning policies;
- log force and tactile feedback as part of the action representation;
- move interface elements to the handle, AR, or voice control;
- develop error compensation for repeated manual offsets;
- extend the approach to welding, spraying, polishing, or other fabrication tasks.
