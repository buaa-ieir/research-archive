## One-sentence summary

CTBC turns “lift the leg immediately after impact” into a learned reflex, allowing small-wheel bipedal robots to climb steps far higher than their wheel radius without vision.

## Problem addressed

Wheeled bipeds are fast and efficient on flat ground but cannot roll over obstacles higher than their wheel radius. Existing stair-climbing systems often depend on large wheels, compliant tires, fixed gaits, or platform-specific tuning, limiting hardware transfer.

The paper asks how contact can provide a reliable trigger for combining a designed motion prior with reinforcement learning across different masses, wheel sizes, tire materials, and actuator bandwidths.

## Main contribution

- A contact-triggered RL task that switches from rolling to stepping upon wheel-obstacle impact.
- Annealed feedforward instruction learning for efficient discovery and internalization of leg lifting.
- A sliding-window contact mechanism for elastic tires.
- Vision-free cross-platform deployment on LimX Tron1 and a 65 kg Cowarobot R0.

## Methodology

![Contact-triggered blind stair-climbing framework with feedforward-trajectory annealing.](/reviews/2026-07-22-CTBC-EN/fig1-framework.png)

*Figure 1. Contact-triggered blind stair-climbing framework with feedforward-trajectory annealing.*

The framework uses an asymmetric PPO actor-critic and an MLP state estimator. The actor observes angular velocity, projected gravity, joint states, and previous actions; the critic also receives body velocity, contact force, and height-scan information during training.

When horizontal contact force exceeds a threshold, a `0.6 s` cosine-shaped feedforward trajectory is injected into the hip and knee actions and blended with the learned policy. The feedforward weight is annealed as training progresses. A two-stage curriculum first allows broad fore-aft foot motion for maneuver discovery, then regularizes foot placement. Domain randomization covers contact, inertia, motors, sensing, delay, and disturbances.

## Experimental setup

![Real-robot escape from a hole and ascent of 16–20 cm steps.](/reviews/2026-07-22-CTBC-EN/fig3-real-world-climbing.png)

*Figure 2. Real-robot escape from a hole and ascent of 16–20 cm steps.*

Ablations compare full CTBC, no feedforward guidance, no contact trigger, and neither component. Each configuration is trained with three seeds for 80,000 iterations and evaluated in 100 randomized environments on 8–22 cm steps.

The 50 Hz hardware policy is tested on a LimX Tron1 in a 10 cm hole, on 16/20 cm steps, and on continuous 20 cm open-gap stairs. The same policy is transferred zero-shot to a 65 kg Cowarobot R0.

## Key results

![Progressive terrain-level training curves for CTBC and its ablations.](/reviews/2026-07-22-CTBC-EN/fig2-ablation-results.png)

*Figure 3. Progressive terrain-level training curves for CTBC and its ablations.*

Full CTBC achieves `100%, 98%, 96%, 86%, and 70%` success on `12, 15, 18, 20, and 22 cm` steps. At 20 cm, removing feedforward guidance reduces success to `58%`, removing contact triggering to `2%`, and removing both to `0%`.

The sub-20 kg Tron1 continuously climbs 20 cm open-gap stairs and escapes a 10 cm hole. Without fine-tuning, the same policy enables the 65 kg R0, equipped with 11 cm-diameter solid tires, to climb 5 cm and 7.5 cm steps.

## Strengths

- The method combines an interpretable contact reflex with RL adaptation.
- Feedforward guidance greatly improves exploration efficiency.
- Cross-platform evidence spans major differences in mass, tire, and bandwidth.
- Ablations clearly show that triggering and instruction are complementary.

## Weaknesses

- The first-stage curriculum creates a persistent front-leg-leading bias.
- A blind policy cannot anticipate obstacles or plan long-horizon routes.
- Repeated impact may affect hardware life but is not systematically analyzed.

## Discussion questions

1. How should contact thresholds adapt to tire stiffness and speed?
2. Can an interpretable reflex be learned without a fixed feedforward trajectory?
3. How should a vision planner arbitrate with the blind reflex layer?
4. How can repeated impact damage be minimized?

## Relevance to our lab

This work is relevant to wheeled humanoids, contact-triggered safety control, and hierarchical embodied-intelligence architectures. Its planner-plus-reflex concept is valuable for industrial environments where vision may be unreliable during sudden contact.

## Possible follow-up ideas

- Combine visual/depth planning with contact-triggered reflexes.
- Use symmetry rewards to remove the leading-leg bias.
- Add impact, tire-deformation, and motor-thermal constraints.
- Test multilevel stairs, random obstacles, and payloads.
