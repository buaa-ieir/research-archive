## One-sentence summary

SAPS blends human teleoperation commands with pretrained VLA actions at the action level, allowing sparse human corrections to recover from out-of-distribution failures without retraining or modifying the base policy.

![SAPS concept](/reviews/2026-06-14-SAPS-EN/fig1-concept.png)

*Figure 1. When a VLA drifts under OOD perturbations, shared-autonomy input can steer the rollout back toward task completion.*

## Problem addressed

VLA and generalist robot policies are increasingly capable, but they remain brittle under out-of-distribution changes in object placement, appearance, and task wording. Pure teleoperation can recover from these failures, but requires continuous precise control. Existing policy-steering methods often require sampler modifications, auxiliary dynamics models, or extra reasoning modules.

The paper asks: **can we steer a frozen pretrained VLA using only action-level shared autonomy?**

## Methodology

![SAPS method](/reviews/2026-06-14-SAPS-EN/fig2-method.png)

*Figure 2. At every control step, the VLA proposes an action, the human may provide a teleoperation action, and an arbitration rule outputs the final blended action.*

SAPS treats the final action as an interpolation between the VLA action and the human action. The paper evaluates three arbitration rules:

- **Takeover**: once human input is active, the human fully controls the robot;
- **Blending**: active human input is mixed with a fixed weight;
- **Cosine similarity arbitration**: the weight changes according to geometric agreement between human and VLA actions.

The key advantage is that SAPS operates after inference. It does not need access to the policy internals, so it can be applied to diffusion, flow-matching, or other VLA action heads as long as the action spaces align.

## Experiments and key results

![LIBERO results](/reviews/2026-06-14-SAPS-EN/fig3-libero-results.png)

*Figure 3. In LIBERO and LIBERO-PRO perturbation settings, sparse human intervention produces large success-rate improvements.*

In controlled LIBERO perturbations, when perturbations become large, π0.5 drops to about 22.9% success, while Blending and Cosine remain around 87.9% and 90.0%. In LIBERO-PRO, autonomous π0.5 achieves only 15.0% mean success, while Blending, Takeover, and Cosine reach 92.6%, 93.2%, and 97.4%, close to pure teleoperation at 98.8%. Crucially, SAPS obtains these gains with much lower human intervention than pure teleoperation.

![Completion time](/reviews/2026-06-14-SAPS-EN/fig4-completion-time.png)

*Figure 4. Shared-autonomy methods reduce completion time relative to both autonomous execution and pure teleoperation in LIBERO-PRO.*

On CALVIN long-horizon chains, Cosine arbitration reaches 94.85% subtask success and compares favorably against steering baselines such as ITPS and DynaGuide.

![CALVIN and real-world results](/reviews/2026-06-14-SAPS-EN/fig5-calvin-real-results.png)

*Figure 5. SAPS improves success in CALVIN and real Franka hardware tasks.*

The real-robot evaluation includes Marker Plate, Close Drawer, and Open Cabinet. The autonomous π0.5 policy struggles, while shared-autonomy variants significantly improve success. Cosine and Blending also reduce human intervention by roughly 30-50% relative to pure teleoperation.

![Qualitative rollouts](/reviews/2026-06-14-SAPS-EN/fig6-qualitative-rollouts.png)

*Figure 6. LIBERO-PRO qualitative rollouts show that human input mainly corrects task direction while the VLA continues to supply low-level manipulation behavior.*

## Strengths

- **Model-agnostic**: no dependence on VLA internals;
- **no retraining**: useful for deployment of frozen policies;
- **sparse human effort**: avoids the burden of continuous teleoperation;
- **broad evaluation** across LIBERO, LIBERO-PRO, CALVIN, and real hardware;
- **interpretable arbitration** through action-direction agreement.

## Weaknesses

- still requires an online human operator;
- human skill and input quality directly affect performance;
- human and policy actions must be compatible in the same action space;
- cosine similarity ignores safety, dynamics, and contact constraints;
- extreme OOD failures may require extended human takeover.

## Discussion questions

1. Is action-level blending sufficient, or do some VLA failures require observation-level or goal-level steering?
2. Could arbitration weights be learned from user workload or policy uncertainty?
3. Should human corrections be stored as new demonstrations for policy improvement?
4. Do safety-critical tasks require a control barrier or projection layer after blending?
5. Can SAPS be combined with corrective language or voice-based high-level guidance?

## Relevance to our lab

SAPS is directly relevant to teleoperation data collection, VLA deployment, and shared autonomy. For glove-to-hand or dexterous teleoperation systems, it suggests a deployment mode in which the robot policy handles most low-level execution while the human supplies sparse corrective input. That both improves success and creates data concentrated around failure recovery.

## Possible follow-up ideas

- test action-level SAPS on a real glove-to-robot-hand platform;
- automatically add human correction segments to a demonstration dataset;
- combine cosine arbitration with tactile feedback, uncertainty, or safety constraints;
- compare intervention rates across different input devices;
- extend action-level steering to bimanual or dexterous high-dimensional action spaces.
