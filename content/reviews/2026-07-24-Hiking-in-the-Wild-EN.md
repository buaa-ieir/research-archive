## One-sentence summary

Hiking in the Wild uses a single-stage pipeline from high-rate egocentric depth histories and proprioception to MoE joint control, together with terrain-edge penalties, Flat Patch Sampling, and AMP priors, to achieve fast and comparatively safe locomotion over real stairs, slopes, platforms, and discrete gaps.

## Problem addressed

Proprioceptive humanoid policies react only after contacting an obstacle and therefore struggle with high platforms, deep gaps, and discrete footholds. Existing perceptive systems often depend on localization, online height maps, or low-rate terrain representations, all of which are vulnerable to pose drift, mapping error, and latency during fast motion.

The paper asks whether a humanoid can map high-frequency depth directly to whole-body joint actions without explicit mapping, while addressing the limited capacity of end-to-end visual policies, edge-induced foot slippage, reward hacking under random velocity commands, and the tension between terrain traversal and natural motion.

## Main contribution

- A single-stage depth-to-joint policy requiring neither explicit localization nor online map reconstruction.
- A 60 Hz depth pipeline, 50 Hz control loop, and temporally strided depth history for fast terrain response.
- A mixture-of-experts policy that reduces interference across terrain types and motion skills.
- A scalable Terrain Edge Detector and Foot Volume Points mechanism for mesh-based foothold safety.
- Flat Patch Sampling, which generates directional velocity commands from feasible foothold goals.
- AMP priors trained from MPC, human motion-capture, and LAFAN motion data.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real deployment on a Unitree G1, supported by real-terrain experiments and systematic ablations.

## Methodology

![End-to-end perceptive locomotion framework in Hiking in the Wild.](/reviews/2026-07-24-Hiking-in-the-Wild-EN/fig1-framework.png)

*Figure 1. Simulated depth and proprioception are encoded by a visual module and an MoE policy to produce joint targets; deployment requires no explicit localization or map reconstruction.*

The task is formulated as a POMDP and optimized with PPO. The actor receives eight-step proprioceptive histories, eight temporally strided depth frames, and velocity commands. A CNN produces a 128-dimensional visual embedding, after which a gate combines four experts to output 29 target joint positions. A PD controller converts these targets into torque commands, while the asymmetric critic additionally observes the true base linear velocity.

Simulation depth is generated by GPU ray casting and augmented with range-dependent noise, contiguous invalid regions, blur, and full-frame out-of-distribution corruption. Real depth is cropped, inpainted, and filtered. Strided history extends the temporal receptive field without processing every image, allowing the policy to infer relative terrain motion.

![Terrain-edge detection and foot-volume safety constraints.](/reviews/2026-07-24-Hiking-in-the-Wild-EN/fig2-edge-safety.png)

*Figure 2. Sharp edges are extracted automatically from triangle meshes, while volume samples inside each foot encourage full-foot placement on stable support.*

During training, adjacent mesh faces with sufficiently large dihedral angles define hazardous edges. Spatial queries measure the penetration of 3D foot-volume samples into these edge structures, with penalties scaled by both penetration distance and foot velocity. This discourages high-speed edge impacts and partial footholds. Flat Patch Sampling identifies locally level mesh regions, selects them as targets, and converts target-relative position into forward and yaw commands, reducing spinning, waiting, and obstacle avoidance as reward-hacking behaviors.

An AMP discriminator supplies style rewards from stable MPC gaits, motion-captured stair and platform skills, and LAFAN running. Walking and running policies are trained separately to avoid gait-mode collapse.

## Experimental setup

Training uses Isaac Sim/Isaac Lab, 4,096 parallel environments, and one RTX 4090. Terrain classes include rough ground, gaps, stairs, platforms, discrete boxes, and slopes, arranged as a 10-by-20 grid of 8-by-8 m sub-terrains. The real platform is a 29-DoF Unitree G1 using its onboard Intel RealSense D435i; depth is cropped to 32×18 pixels, and inference runs on a Jetson Orin NX.

Each real terrain-gait condition is tested ten times. Ablations remove depth history, position-based commands, MoE, AMP, depth itself, or replace high-rate depth with a 10 Hz height map. A separate study measures how the terrain-edge penalty changes success and effective landing area.

## Key results

![Real-world deployment over platforms, grass slopes, stairs, and discrete gaps.](/reviews/2026-07-24-Hiking-in-the-Wild-EN/fig3-real-deployment.png)

*Figure 3. Walking and running deployments of the Unitree G1 across diverse real terrain.*

The system reaches `2.5 m/s`, traverses a `32 cm` platform and a `50 cm` gap, and operates for four continuous minutes over stairs and flat ground without a fall or intervention. Walking success is close to 100% on platforms and stairs; running onto a high platform reaches roughly 70%, while running on grass reaches roughly 100%.

![Effects of edge penalties on footholds, real slipping, and edge detection on unseen meshes.](/reviews/2026-07-24-Hiking-in-the-Wild-EN/fig4-edge-results.png)

*Figure 4. Edge-aware training moves the support area inward and transfers to new mesh topologies.*

On Small Box terrain, the complete method reaches `99.09%`, compared with `93.17%` without the edge penalty; the stair-descent landing-area score improves from `0.87` to `0.94`. More strongly, removing depth history, AMP, high-rate depth, or depth entirely yields only `1.66%`, `0%`, `15.28%`, and `3.33%` on Small Box. Removing MoE or position-based commands gives `84.51%` and `90.68%`. These results support the combined importance of high-rate perception, motion priors, and explicit foothold-safety training.

## Strengths

- Direct depth-to-control learning avoids error propagation across localization, mapping, and control modules.
- High perception and control rates support fast platform traversal and continuous foothold adjustment.
- Edge detection and foot-volume constraints are geometrically interpretable and mesh-agnostic.
- Flat Patch Sampling attacks reward hacking at the task-generation level.
- Ablations cover temporal vision, MoE, AMP, command generation, edge safety, and low-rate mapping.
- The system deploys with the robot's stock camera and onboard computation.

## Weaknesses

- A single forward camera limits backward and lateral locomotion; lateral command velocity is fixed to zero.
- Walking and running require separate policies rather than one unified gait policy.
- Flat Patch targets exist only in simulation, and the deployed system has no global navigation or long-term terrain memory.
- Geometric edges do not capture friction, softness, wetness, or deformability.
- The 32×18 depth input is coarse, while each real condition has only ten trials and no confidence intervals.
- There is no same-hardware comparison against other perceptive locomotion methods.
- Falling terminates training, so the robot cannot recover autonomously.
- Several PPO, randomization, and geometric hyperparameters remain unspecified.

## Discussion questions

1. Do the MoE components learn interpretable terrain or gait specializations, and can gating errors create abrupt actions?
2. Does AMP's decisive effect on Small Box arise from visual naturalness, swing-leg stability, or a stronger state-distribution constraint?
3. How should the temporal stride of depth history adapt to motion speed and sensor latency?
4. How can simulation-only Flat Patch goals connect to deployed local planning or global navigation?
5. Can fall prediction, a safety shield, and autonomous recovery be added without sacrificing reactivity?

## Relevance to our lab

The paper is directly relevant to Unitree G1 rough-terrain locomotion, visual control, AMP priors, and sim-to-real deployment. Its terrain-edge objective offers a concrete response to partial-foot stair contacts and simulation-to-hardware slipping. The single-stage visual policy also provides a useful counterpoint to privileged-teacher distillation. Adding recovery motions to the prior and coordinating locomotion and recovery through MoE or skill latents could lead to a reset-free unified controller.

## Possible follow-up ideas

- Use multi-view depth and temporal transformers for omnidirectional locomotion.
- Extend Flat Patch goals to local paths or sequences of future footholds.
- Jointly model edges, contact area, plantar pressure, and friction.
- Condition gating on sensor confidence and add an online safety filter.
- Unify walking, running, jumping, and recovery through skill latents.
- Evaluate dynamic obstacles, long-horizon outdoor traversal, and cross-robot transfer.
