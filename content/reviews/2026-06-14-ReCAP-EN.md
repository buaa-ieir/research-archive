## One-sentence summary

ReCAP learns the source-to-target embodiment gap once, then expands a frozen VLA policy to unseen tasks by adding inexpensive demonstrations to a retrieval pool rather than collecting new robot data and retraining.

![ReCAP overview](/reviews/2026-06-14-ReCAP-EN/fig1-overview.png)

*Figure 1. Conventional adaptation repeats robot teleoperation and fine-tuning; ReCAP keeps the policy frozen and expands a low-cost demonstration pool.*

## Problem addressed

Adapting a VLA policy to each new task commonly requires target-robot teleoperation and task-specific optimization. The paper estimates that robot demonstrations are roughly 18 times slower to acquire than comparable human-hand demonstrations, while fine-tuning a video-based policy can cost about 24 GPU-hours per task. It asks whether task knowledge can instead live in an external memory that is updated at deployment.

## Main contribution

- ReCAP, a retrieval-conditioned action policy that absorbs new tasks without parameter updates;
- one-time training on paired target- and source-embodiment demonstrations;
- a residual action formulation in which retrieval supplies coarse task progress and the policy corrects embodiment-specific dynamics;
- integration with Cosmos Policy, whose future-image objective encourages visually consistent corrections;
- cross-embodiment evaluation on PushT, RoboTwin 2.0, and a physical robot.

## Methodology

At each control step, the query observation retrieves a matching source trajectory chunk using language, object pose, proprioception, and visual features. The retrieved action provides a high-level motion prior. The policy predicts only the residual needed to execute that motion on the target embodiment.

![ReCAP method](/reviews/2026-06-14-ReCAP-EN/fig2-method.png)

*Figure 2. A retrieved source state-action chunk and the current observation condition a world-action model that predicts the target action and next observation.*

The world-action model jointly optimizes action and future-state prediction. Retrieval indicates what progression to follow, while image prediction provides a dense signal about whether that progression is physically and visually consistent on the target robot. Deployment adds new source-side trajectories to the index without updating the policy.

## Experiments and key results

On cross-embodiment PushT, the policy is trained only at ±45° and evaluated on seven unseen goal angles. Growing the pool raises average unseen-angle success monotonically from 6.0% without retrieval to 34.9%. Retrieval raises action-only π0.5 from 6.6% to 25.1%; adding the world-model image objective reaches 34.9%.

![PushT results](/reviews/2026-06-14-ReCAP-EN/fig3-pusht-results.png)

*Figure 3. Pool growth, backbone comparisons, and objective ablations show complementary benefits from retrieval and future-image prediction.*

On RoboTwin 2.0, ReCAP obtains 43.5% success on seen tasks and 31.5% on unseen tasks, compared with 32.5%/4.0% for target-only training and 26.0% unseen success for direct retrieval execution.

![RoboTwin results](/reviews/2026-06-14-ReCAP-EN/fig4-robotwin-results.png)

*Figure 4. The learned residual corrects collisions and grasp-orientation errors in retrieved source-robot trajectories.*

The real-robot model is trained on 25 paired open-cabinet demonstrations. Adding ten human demonstrations per held-out task raises bottle placement from 10% to 80% and cabinet closing from 0% to 30%.

![Real-robot results](/reviews/2026-06-14-ReCAP-EN/fig5-real-robot.png)

*Figure 5. A frozen policy executes held-out cabinet-closing and bottle-placement behaviors by conditioning on newly indexed human trajectories.*

## Strengths

- Clearly separates adaptation to a new embodiment from adaptation to a new task.
- Demonstrates retrieval gains with both an action-only VLA and a world-action model.
- Pool-progression experiments directly test the central no-retraining claim.
- Combines controlled analysis, multi-task simulation, and physical-robot evidence.
- Frames deployment cost in practical data-collection and compute terms.

## Weaknesses

- Source and target embodiments require a compatible end-effector action representation.
- The pool must contain state-action trajectories rather than unprocessed videos.
- Retrieval relies on engineered multimodal descriptors and privileged object poses in parts of the evaluation.
- Residual correction may break under large temporal or dynamical mismatch.
- The physical evaluation covers only three tasks with ten trials each.

## Discussion questions

1. How can retrieval work between structurally different hands and grippers?
2. How will latency and retrieval errors scale with a much larger experience pool?
3. Can actions be recovered automatically from ordinary human videos?
4. Does future-image prediction learn dynamics correction or mainly visual regularization?
5. What should the policy do when retrieval confidence is low?

## Relevance to our lab

The paper directly connects to teleoperation data collection, motion retargeting, and stable robot-side control. It suggests concentrating expensive robot demonstrations on learning embodiment differences, then using cheaper human or alternate-robot demonstrations to broaden task coverage. In a glove-to-robot-hand system, retrieved trajectories could provide high-level motion priors while the online controller handles limits, contacts, and timing.

## Possible follow-up ideas

- Learn embodiment-invariant action or contact tokens.
- Couple retrieval confidence with safety filters and human takeover.
- Retrieve jointly over video, force, and tactile signals.
- Develop hierarchical retrieval for long-horizon tasks.
- Reject retrieved chunks with poor dynamic feasibility or temporal alignment.
